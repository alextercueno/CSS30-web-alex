Clean Water and Sanitation
PART 1

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) listed clean water and sanitation as one of its seventeen (17) goals. Lin, Yang, & Xu (2022) and Ejiohuo, Onyeaka, Akinsemolu, Nwabor, Siyanbola, Tamasiga, & Al-Sharify (2025) believe that water is essential for sustaining life, not only humans, but also ecosystems. Dr. Gil (W12 Zoom Recording) said that SDGs should be taken seriously and explained that it concerns uplifting and improving the welfare of the nation state, providing comfortable life.

According to United Nations (UN, 2001), in a press release statement made by Secretary General Kofi Annan on World Water Day, since safe water is a human need, it is also considered as a human right as having unsafe water compromises the health of all people and is an affront to humanity. This is supported by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2023), stating that safe water is also the practice of good hygiene, which prevents diseases and infections; in 2022, only 73 percent of the population has access to safe water, free from contamination (WHO). Furthermore, nearly two and a half billion live without access to safe water as well as basic sanitation (UN).

In addition, the use of unsafe water is also a factor in increasing diseases and death in the world (UN, 2001). Moreover, the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA, 2025) explained that unsafe drinking water can cause health effects such as, but not limited to, intestinal problems, nervous and reproductive effects, and chronic diseases. Over 2 billion people live in countries where water is scarce, factoring climate changes and population growth in 2021 (WHO, 2023).

It is weird to be sitting on the other side of the world, also struggling but not as underprivileged as those who are living in the poverty line. In the Philippines alone, I have observed dirty and unsafe water everywhere. Near my dorm in Manila, there is a family living on the side of the road, specifically on the pavement. They are living with an old pedicab, covered with old tarpaulins and used kartons. They seem to be fine, not until there are heavy rainfalls continuously. I have seen them struggling to be inside the pedicab and avoiding to get wet from the rainfall. It is kind of disheartening that these kind of things are happening; I want to help but I am powerless. So, it is kind of a dilemma for me if I should give them alms or if I should just continue on my day. It is also important to be involved in this kind of civic engagement, as long as it is a personal decision and should not be caused by peer pressure (Dr. Gil, W10 Zoom Summary).

Once, I saw them washing their clothes with just a gallon of water. Unsure if it is clean, and if it will suffice them for a long time since water in Manila is a bit expensive compared to provincial areas. Dr. Gil (W10 Zoom Summary) mentioned that it is important to be safe and follow health protocols. However, clean water is still unaffordable to a lot of people, including those who are in poverty line.

According to United Nations Development Programme (UNDP, n.d.), a lot of countries are experiencing water problems, including their access, wherein it is projected to worsen by 2050; one in four will experience shortages of water.

With United Nations’ SDGs, it has been helping to achieve this goal since 2015. First, safely managed and drinking water has increased to 74 percent in 2024, from 68 percent in 2015. Second, safely managed sanitation has increased to 58 percent in 2024, from its 48 percent in 2015. Lastly, basic hygiene services had the best improvement from 66 percent in 2015, to 80 percent in 2024.

This means that the SDG’s efforts in improving the clean water and sanitation are effective. To think that there are 16 other goals they are focusing on improving, its efforts are showing great results from the past decade. However, only 39 percent of the population had access to safe sanitation in 2015, and the rest lacked thereof (UNDP, n.d.). Additionally, large portions of water wastes are left untreated, making it unsuitable for consumption (UNDP).

The main message is a lot of people are dying and often overlooked especially those in poorer regions. Thankfully, there are organizations that are willing to help and improve clean water and sanitation on these regions, without expecting anything in return, aside from the good results from their goals which is successful.

PART 2

The Filipino people living in slums are also included in the percentage of those who cannot access clean water and sanitation. Their shelters alone are packed together, limiting privacy. Their food mostly came from leftovers, or worse from garbage bags, and then just reheated for consumption just to survive the day. Some probably just let it be, and sit in hunger throughout the day. Likewise, their access to clean water and sanitation is impossible, unless otherwise someone gives them alms, or water directly; 20 million Filipinos have no access to sanitation and proper sewage facilities (Rappler, 2013). As from what I have observed, it mostly came from individual donations and kind-hearted people, including donation drives. It rarely came from the government, which is ironic and confusing at the same time. Since the government should be the first in helping these people and addressing the situation.

One issue to consider is the contamination of water. Lin et al. (2022) described water pollution as being mostly seen in industry, agriculture, and especially in sewage treatment facilities. Focusing on sewage treatment facilities, the sewage system in the Philippines is unkempt. Wherever you look at, you will be able to see a sewer canal that is open and full of garbage, debris, etc. Even if the place is unpopulous, sewers are still dirty, smells bad, and mixed up trashes. World Health Organization (WHO, 2017) defined wastewater as composition of liquid waste from bathrooms, kitchen, and laundry, including economic and institutional wastes. These still remains to be treated or cured before being eligible for human consumption. Furthermore, these wastes may contain toxic and hazardous chemicals as well as dangerous organisms (WHO).

From what I envision, treating wastewater and proper sewage facilities are easy to create, fix, and modify to accommodate modernity. Years are passing and our sewage facilities in the Philippines are still outdated and overlooked. Well, I can always see ongoing maintenance on water suppliers alongside the manila streets. However, these maintenance are from private companies fixing their water system to accommodate their customers. Unfortunately, it is not government-operated maintenance fixing our sewage systems. Meaning, the sewage systems remain to be unkempt for longer periods of time. WHO (2017), explained that some Filipinos openly defecated in sewer canals due to limited access to sanitation which factored to the deaths of 55 Filipinos per day.

For me, simply by focusing on these sewage systems can improve overall welfare and health not only of the privileged residents, but also of the biggest stakeholders which are the residents living in poverty, and by the slums of Manila. First, they can clean open sewer canals and removing its debris, dirt, and trash. By doing so, it can improve the flow of the wastewater as well as the smell coming out of it. Second, they can close these open sewer canals to reduce foul odor emitted from it and contamination, as well as improving the sidewalks. Less odor would improve the walkway and reduce the swerving of many Filipinos while walking alongside these canals. Lastly, they can treat the wastewater properly and quickly, improving the sewage systems.

Paradoxically, the Philippines’ government has set a goal of improving water quality by 2020 through the National Sewerage and Septage Management Program (NSSMP) (WHO, 2017). However, it is already 2025 and there seems to be no improvement in sewage system at all, or at least noticeable differences.

Furthermore, the World Health Organization together with the Asian Development Bank (ADB) and Department of Health (DOH) tries the Sanitation Safety Planning (SSP) as a stepping stone in risk assessment in sanitation from households during 2014. SSP is a factor in achieving following SDGs for Clean Water and Sanitation by 2030: reducing the deaths caused by water pollution, proper sanitation and hygiene, and improved water quality by reducing untreated wastewaters.

Another issue is a lot of Filipinos have no access to clean water. Wateroam (2020) stated that 1 in 10 Filipinos still cannot access clean water sources. As confusing as it gets, the earlier mentions of the Philippine government partnering with other organizations to improve the water ways in the Philippines has been stagnant, or at least its results were only viable on its first months and left overlooked after, like any other government projects.
